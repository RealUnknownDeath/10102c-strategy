Score while controlling the toggles

A shared match plan for 10102C and Red-1. Understand each robot’s job, recognize what changes, and explain when to change plans.

Discussion draft · October 5, 2026

Read with the updated analysis

This is the original discussion sequence. N2/N4 are neutral goals and have no knockdown fallback. MID placement must finish before the final ten seconds. A lead is conditional on both opponents’ remaining options, not just the toggle holder.

The plan in one sentence

Take quick scoring early, build alliance color, and finish on productive routes near the toggles; if an opponent commits to defending one area, use the space they leave elsewhere.

We do not need to chase every toggle change immediately. We do need to understand which stacks it affects, whether the opponent can keep it, and how the score changes if we leave it alone.

This is our first strategy, including the branches discussed so far. The illustrated comeback depends on scoring, travel time, and opponent reactions; it is not an automatic win.

1. Speak the same field language

10102C is us. Red-1 is our teammate. Opponents are Blue 1 and Blue 2. Toggles are Top, Left, Right, and Down from the fixed view below. Keep that orientation when discussing positions.

R1 and R2 are our alliance goals. B1 and B2 are the blue alliance goals. N1 through N4 are the four surrounding neutral goals; MID is the center goal. Red arrows show our intended travel, blue arrows show an opponent’s possible response, and green marks highlight an option or proposed operating area.

2. Use the autonomous opening

The baseline begins after an autonomous win. Our expectation for competitive autos is around 2–3 pins on each alliance goal, with occasional misses. A five-pin autonomous routine is a possible 10102C target, not something this plan assumes is already reliable.

At driver control, look for the remaining upright yellow cup-pin stacks. They are attractive because they can be quick pickups, especially for a claw robot without an intake. Score the easy ones onto alliance goals while the trip is worthwhile. This is an opportunity rule, not an instruction to chase every yellow across the field.

Take the quick upright stacks and name the goal you are using. Do not turn the cleanup into a long detour.

We have not yet built the alternate opening for losing autonomous, a tied auto, or a badly missed routine. Do not pretend the same field state exists in those cases.

3. Build color early and return near the toggle late

After the easy cleanup, build alliance color on the nearby neutral goal toward your starting side. The teammate can run a similar scoring pattern or continue loading alliance color. Exact assignments depend on the pair and still need to be agreed.

Use earlier match time for the farther scoring routes. If the opponent does not take the toggle during that window, spend roughly the last 30 seconds scoring carefully on the goals beside it. The timing is situational: the point is to remain productive while close enough to defend.

Use nearby scoring and watch the approach to your toggle. Defend when threatened; do not park unnecessarily while scoring remains available.

If they take a toggle early and leave, reclaim the surrounding space through your scoring route first. Flip it back when the opportunity is convenient. Ownership of a toggle and control of the space around it are different. The opponent must keep contesting that space to prevent a cheap recovery.

Nearby supply can run out late. We still need a specific response for that, rather than assuming there will always be another local cycle.

4. When Blue 1 camps Toggle Left

R1, and possibly N2, may already hold yellows from auto. R1 may also have received additional driver-control yellows. Once Blue 1 establishes control of Left, stop adding yellows to R1. Do not feed the advantage you are trying to overcome.

You still have R2, N4, and MID. One opponent at Left does not shut down all of your scoring. The question is how much value they gain from holding Left compared with what we gain elsewhere.

When we are ahead

10102C’s proposed order is N4 first, MID if Blue 2’s path allows it, then R2. Building our color on N4 may reduce easy scoring opportunities available to the opponent. Do not cross Blue 2’s route unnecessarily to force a MID cycle.

The exact ordering is a detail we can tune later. The larger decision is to keep scoring while the opponent commits a robot to Left. Being ahead plus having open options is favorable, but we still need to protect the lead rather than assume it will last.

Stop feeding R1 yellows. Use N4, an available MID route, or R2. Revisit Left when an opportunity appears.

5. When we are behind

The proposed comeback changes the teammate’s job. Red-1 scores a couple of alliance-colored pieces up north on N1 or N2, then travels to Toggle Down. 10102C builds yellows in the south while Red-1 protects their value by defending Down.

Red-1 confirms coverage. 10102C takes the scoring job. The defender should still score when a short, recoverable cycle is available.

This gives us a way to catch up. It also commits a robot to coverage, so our scoring rate and remaining time matter. The next branch handles Blue 2 trying to interfere with the scorer.

6. Make the scorer and defender switch jobs when needed

If Blue 2 approaches to stop 10102C scoring in the south, 10102C can move north and attempt productive scoring there. If Blue 2 follows, Red-1 gains room to score near Down. If Blue 2 stays south, 10102C gains room up north. We cannot force the chase; we react to what Blue 2 actually does.

10102C moves toward useful northern scoring. Red-1 checks that Blue 2 actually follows before treating the south as open.

This is the role switch to recognize: a robot that was covering a toggle becomes the nearby scorer, while its partner moves the pressure elsewhere. Red-1 still watches Blue 1 at Left.

7. If Blue 1 leaves Left to attack Down

This is the longer example from our discussion. Its purpose is to explain a potential response, not to make every match depend on this exact sequence.

Position A — score inside a recoverable area

Red-1 can visit the lower loader and score near R2. The green area is our proposed working region, redrawn for clarity. The reasoning is that Red-1 has a direct route back toward Down while Blue 1 must navigate around the goal area.

Position B — reverse toward Down as Blue 1 commits

If Blue 1 leaves Left, Red-1 stops extending the scoring route and returns toward Down. The proposed advantage is a straighter retreat, potentially by reversing, rather than a long turn around the goals. Notice the commitment early; do not wait until the opponent reaches the toggle.

Red-1 takes the short return. 10102C watches for an opening at Left instead of both robots chasing the same contest.

Position C — the contest shifts to Down

In the expected contact position, Red-1 is on the R2 side of Down and Blue 1 is on the N4 side. They face each other across the toggle area. With Blue 1 committed there, Left is no longer occupied by that robot.

Position D — take Left if the return is available

10102C retakes Left while Red-1 holds Down. Our intended finish is for both robots to defend their toggles and preserve the scoring advantage. This is the desired outcome of the branch, not an inevitable result.

10102C confirms the return is open and takes Left. Red-1 continues covering Down. Both reassess the score before committing to a hold-only finish.

The sequence needs practical testing: how early Red-1 must react, whether the straight retreat remains open, which side Blue 1 actually approaches from, and whether 10102C can return while Blue 2 is nearby. Specific contact tactics also need to fit the current game rules.

8. Choose the finish from the score and toggle state

If we control the relevant toggles and the resulting score is enough, defending may be the best use of the remaining time. If we are still behind, two stationary defenders do not solve the deficit: someone must create more value.

If blue still holds Left and the yellows on R1 benefit blue, deliberately knocking down that yellow stack is the discussed fallback. Either robot could do it if it has access and enough time. The original idea was the final five seconds; that is an execution possibility, not a rule to delay the decision until five seconds remain.

Name the goal and confirm why its yellow stack hurts us. One robot takes the job; the other preserves useful scoring or toggle control.

Before the finish, answer three things together: Who owns the toggles that matter? Which stacks change the result? Who can reach the necessary action in time? Avoid both robots abandoning coverage for the same cleanup.

The goal is understanding the decisions, not memorizing a sequence that only works against one opponent response. Red-1 should be able to explain these points in their own words while looking at the diagrams.

What to look at before deciding

Score, time, toggles, opponents, and the next useful job. A toggle changing color is information; it is not automatically a reason to abandon a productive scoring cycle.

What to practice first

  1. Identify every goal and toggle without hesitation.
  2. Explain whether an opponent is passing through or committing to hold an area.
  3. Run one scoring cycle and identify the earliest cue to return.
  4. Explain the role switch with the opponent both following and refusing to follow.
  5. Choose between holding, scoring, retaking, and clearing from a stated score and remaining time, then explain why.

Later application to the practice game

The Override and Pinnacle bots should eventually execute complete plans, coordinate two-bot alliances, and support a human’s lead. Simple commands such as toggle coverage could let the human assign a role. Different strategies should create different practice problems.

The user reports the game currently lacks an autonomous winner. Its starting scenarios need inspection so the practice opening can reflect auto outcomes and resulting stacks. Bot evaluation should check strategic behavior and useful human practice, not only bot-versus-bot wins. No game implementation or publication is part of this playbook draft.
